# Local LLM or provider subscription?

Two ways of using artificial intelligence are becoming increasingly common: subscribing to an online provider, or running AI models locally on your own hardware.

At first glance, the provider subscription looks like the cheaper option. For users who are not limited by political, legal, or organisational constraints, there are attractive offers on the market, including from Chinese providers.

But that comparison changes when the time comes to buy new hardware.

Most users whose needs go beyond simple office work already invest in a mid-range or high-end notebook. That makes hardware renewal a good moment to ask a wider question: should the next computer also be able to run AI locally?

Kashif Mehmood’s article, “614 vs 273 GB/s: Why a MacBook Still Beats NVIDIA’s New RTX Spark at Local LLMs”, explains the technical background well.

For non-technical readers, the key point is simple: as of late August 2026, Apple Silicon still offers a strong advantage over a traditional PC with an NVIDIA RTX graphics card for local AI work, both in performance and in value for money.

This may change soon. From autumn 2026, several manufacturers are expected to release machines based on NVIDIA’s RTX Spark processor. These systems should reduce the gap and offer Windows users a serious local AI option at a more acceptable price.

There is one important detail: this means Windows 11 on ARM, which is not directly compatible with the current Intel x64 Windows ecosystem. That may sound like a limitation, but it could also be an opportunity to move beyond some of the legacy constraints of the Windows platform.

The practical result is clear. A modern notebook can now be fast and reliable for ordinary work, while also running many AI tasks locally at no extra subscription cost, using tools such as Ollama.

With recent local models such as Qwen3.8:27B, many code-generation tasks can be carried out without sending data to an external provider. That brings clear advantages for confidentiality, discretion, and data protection.

The same applies beyond software development. For photography enthusiasts, Black Forest Labs provides models such as Flux.2-klein-4b, which can generate images and correct photographs in less than a minute.

Local AI is not free in every sense. It still requires some maintenance: models, tools, and environments must be kept up to date. But the trade-off is attractive. It can reduce recurring costs and limit dependence on external providers and, indirectly, on the governments under which those providers operate.

The final point is responsibility. Running AI locally does not remove the duty of care. AI agents must still be monitored, controlled, and prevented from creating risks for third parties.

Local AI is not always the right answer. But when new hardware is already part of the plan, it deserves serious consideration.
